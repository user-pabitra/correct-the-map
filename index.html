Resolution A/80/L.104

What the UN actually adopted

A non-binding resolution introduced by Togo, co-sponsored by France, and backed by the African Union, formally adopted by recorded vote on 4 September 2026 during the 80th Session of the UN General Assembly.

164 In Favour
1 Against - United States
6 Abstained
22 Not Voting / Absent

Abstentions (UN press record): Estonia · Georgia · Lithuania · Republic of Moldova · Serbia · Ukraine

What it says

Encourages governments, schools, international organisations, and technology companies to use Equal Earth and other equal-area projections where relative size matters. Frames the historical Mercator distortion as an "act of cognitive justice and memorial reparation" requiring correction. Invites UNESCO, UNDP and UN bodies to support implementation.

What it does not do

Nothing becomes mandatory. The resolution is non-binding and explicitly states it does not challenge the use of Mercator "for maritime and aerial navigation, for which it remains fully suited." The EU noted on record it has no bearing on sovereignty, territorial status, or borders.

France acts immediately

Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot announced at the Sorbonne that France will abandon Mercator for its official world maps, becoming the first major power to act on the resolution. He stated: "Correcting a distorted representation is already an act of truth."

The dissents & caveats

US Ambassador Mike Waltz called the text part of "a much larger and more radical ideological project" and compared such resolutions to "barnacles on our work." Ukraine's Andriy Melnyk abstained, citing concern that Equal Earth maps depict Russian-occupied Ukrainian territory in a manner it found unacceptable.

"Maps shape our understanding of the world. They are therefore never neutral. When they present a distorted image of our planet, they risk perpetuating inaccurate representations passed down through generations."
- Robert Dussey, Foreign Minister of Togo, introducing Resolution A/80/L.104
"This initiative will bring an end to misrepresentation in classrooms, media and international institutions, helping ensure that the world map reflects Africa's actual geographical scale."
- African Union Commission, statement on adoption, 4 Sep 2026
The Mathematical Problem

Mercator inflates area by sec²φ - that is arithmetic, not politics

Mercator (1569) was a navigational masterpiece: a straight line on it is a constant compass bearing (rhumb line). The unavoidable mathematical price is that scale grows as 1/cos φ in every direction, so area inflates by its square. Gauss's Theorema Egregium guarantees no flat map can fix everything at once.

Latitude Linear scale factor Mercator area inflation Equal Earth area
The key insight: Africa straddles the Equator (0°), where Mercator has zero distortion. Greenland sits at 60–83°N, where Mercator inflates area 4× to 10×. Africa is in reality 14× larger than Greenland - on a Mercator map they can look roughly equivalent.
Gauss's Theorema Egregium (1827) proves that no flat map can perfectly preserve all of area, shape, distance, and direction simultaneously. Every projection chooses its trade-off. Mercator chose angles. Equal Earth chooses area.

Major distortion examples (Mercator vs. reality)

  • Greenland appears ≈ same size as Africa - reality: Africa is 14× larger
  • Alaska appears ≈ same size as Australia - reality: Australia is 4.5× larger
  • Russia appears larger than Africa - reality: Russia is only 56% of Africa's area
  • Europe appears larger than South America - reality: South America is larger
  • DRC alone (2.3 M km²) is bigger than Greenland - not apparent on Mercator

The Equal Earth Projection · EPSG:8857

The cartographers who built a fairer map

The Equal Earth projection was published in 2018 in the peer-reviewed International Journal of Geographical Information Science and registered as EPSG:8857. It is free, open, and already implemented in PROJ, QGIS, GDAL, ArcGIS, D3.js, and Cartopy.

BŠ
Bojan Šavrič
Esri · Portland, Oregon

Slovenian projection engineer at Esri. Led the mathematical design - a Robinson-like aesthetic constrained to exact area preservation via polynomial parametric equations. Also designed the Natural Earth projection used by National Geographic.

TP
Tom Patterson
US National Park Service

Veteran cartographer known for shaded relief and natural-looking terrain rendering. Shaped the projection's visual balance and familiar appearance - ensuring it would be accepted in classrooms, not just academic journals. Retired from NPS in 2019.

BJ
Bernhard Jenny
Monash University, Australia

Professor of geovisualisation at Monash University. Long-time collaborator with Šavrič, co-authored the 2018 IJGIS paper. Specialist in map aesthetics and projection design software. His lab created Flex Projector, used to prototype the design.

Why Equal Earth, not Gall–Peters?

The Gall–Peters projection (1885/1973) is also equal-area but severely compresses shapes - countries look stretched and unfamiliar, which limited educational acceptance for decades. Equal Earth was deliberately designed in 2018, partly in response to that controversy, to be equal-area and aesthetically appealing. The curved sides suggest the globe. Shapes are recognisable. NASA's Goddard Institute for Space Studies adopted it immediately on publication.

Why Mercator still has legitimate uses

Rhumb lines (constant compass bearing routes) plot as straight lines on Mercator - still essential for aviation charts and marine navigation. Web Mercator (EPSG:3857) enables seamless, infinitely zoomable tile maps used by Google Maps, OpenStreetMap, and every major mapping API. The UN resolution explicitly carves these uses out. The point is not to ban Mercator; it is to stop using it as the default comparison map when relative size matters.

NASA GISS - the Goddard Institute for Space Studies - adopted Equal Earth as its default global map projection immediately after the 2018 publication, using it for climate science visualisations. The projection is available in ArcGIS Pro 2.3+, ArcGIS Desktop 10.7+, QGIS (via PROJ), GDAL, D3.js, and Python's Cartopy.
457 Years

How we got here

From a navigator's tool in 1569 to a diplomatic resolution in 2026 - the story of a map and its consequences.

  • 1569 Gerardus Mercator publishes his world chart The Flemish cartographer creates a cylindrical projection that makes rhumb lines straight - a breakthrough for ocean navigation. The distortion of polar regions is a deliberate, known trade-off, not an oversight.
  • 1855 James Gall proposes an equal-area alternative Scottish clergyman James Gall presents an equal-area cylindrical projection at the Glasgow meeting of the British Association. It would be largely ignored for over a century.
  • 1973 Arno Peters reignites the size-perception debate German historian Arno Peters presents the Gall–Peters projection as a correction of Mercator's "Eurocentric bias," triggering a decades-long cartographic and political controversy. Despite equal-area accuracy, the stretched shapes limit classroom adoption.
  • 1998 National Geographic adopts Winkel Tripel The American Cartographic Association had already recommended moving away from Mercator; National Geographic's adoption of Winkel Tripel marks mainstream cartography's formal break with Mercator for reference maps.
  • 2018 Equal Earth projection published & registered Šavrič, Patterson and Jenny publish the Equal Earth paper in the International Journal of Geographical Information Science (doi:10.1080/13658816.2018.1504949). EPSG:8857 is registered. NASA GISS adopts it immediately. The projection debuts at the North American Cartographic Information Society conference.
  • Feb 2026 African Union endorses "Correct the Map" at its Assembly The African Union Assembly formally endorses the Correct the Map initiative - a campaign led by Africa No Filter and Speak Up Africa - authorising Togo to lead the push at the UN General Assembly during the 80th Session.
  • 4 Sep 2026 UN General Assembly adopts Resolution A/80/L.104 - 164–1–6 The historic vote passes with 164 in favour, 1 against (United States), and 6 abstentions (Estonia, Georgia, Lithuania, Republic of Moldova, Serbia, Ukraine). France announces on the same day it will abandon Mercator for its official maps. Togo sets a deadline to update school geography materials by end of 2026.
GIS & Geospatial Implications

What this means for geospatial professionals

Projection confusion costs real money

Computing areas in geographic (EPSG:4326) or Web Mercator (EPSG:3857) coordinate systems is a common GIS error that produces wildly incorrect results. A polygon representing Greenland computed in EPSG:4326 degrees will give a meaningless area. EPSG:8857 (Equal Earth) is the correct CRS for any global area computation - it is equal-area by definition.

The default CRS problem

Most web mapping stacks default to EPSG:3857 (Web Mercator) for display and EPSG:4326 for data storage. Neither is appropriate for area measurement. The resolution effectively endorses a three-CRS discipline: 4326 for storage, 3857 for tiles, and 8857 for any visualisation or measurement where relative size is the message.

Education and public perception

A 2020 study (ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information, >130,000 respondents) found that exposure to Mercator maps measurably shapes geographic size perception. Respondents systematically overestimated the size of high-latitude countries. The UN resolution targets precisely this educational context.

Technology companies invited

The resolution explicitly invites technology companies - read: Google, Microsoft, Apple, Mapbox, Esri - to adopt equal-area projections where comparative size matters. No obligation exists, but the diplomatic signal is clear. Esri's ArcGIS has included EPSG:8857 since version 2.3; Bojan Šavrič, who designed Equal Earth, is an Esri employee.

Practical checklist for GIS teams: (1) Never compute areas in EPSG:4326 or 3857. (2) Use EPSG:8857 for global thematic maps where size is the message. (3) Use EPSG:3857 only for tile/navigation display. (4) Use EPSG:4326 for data storage and interchange. (5) When publishing choropleth or proportional symbol maps, state the projection.
